## Executive judgment

Moore is particularly strong in **relating the geometry of vault cells to the continuity of the surrounding architectural system**. For MP-03 he supplies a rigorous qualitative test, not a ready-made N10 plan: an exceptional vault should accommodate the surrounding bay rhythm, and the supporting piers/arcade must genuinely correspond to the rib organization. **The most direct precedent for a special compartment preserving ordinary adjacent vault rhythm is his discussion of the apse at Noyon, Chartres, and Amiens (pp. 163–165, figs. 85–88).**

No historical case in this book was verified as an exact counterpart to N10's three lower spans, 4032-unit transition, equal-pair station, or Option C paired-canopy configuration. Do not present these as sourced from Moore.

## Primary-source evidence and interpretive use

### Finding M01 — Distinguish vault cells from architectural bays

**Chapter II, pp. 48–49, fig. 15**: Moore reconstructs the insertion of an *intermediate transverse rib* into a large square compartment at the Abbaye-aux-Hommes, Caen. In his account, this subdivides lateral triangular surfaces of a quadripartite design to produce a sexpartite vault. The web-cell count and the larger support rhythm are not identical categories.

**Application:** Three internal surfaces or branches can potentially be read under two primary architectural canopies, *but only when the primary arcade, ribs, and supports make the two-part hierarchy visible*. This is not validation of C-P2; the existing third arcade beat is still legible.

### Finding M02 — Different vault families can coexist

**Chapter III, p. 108:** Moore states that in the choir of **St-Leu-d’Esserent** quadripartite and sexpartite forms occur **side by side** and are contemporaneous. **Chapter VI, pp. 196–197, fig. 106:** he reports that the early rebuilt **Canterbury choir** combines one quadripartite and two sexpartite compartments.

**Application:** Uniformity need not mean total replication of a vault formula. A crossing-adjacent exception may be coherent. **Limit:** these do not prove that a visible three-bay sequence will be aesthetically compatible with a paired arcade.

### Finding M03 — Support-vault mismatch is the danger

**Chapter IV, pp. 112–114:** Moore objects to what he regards as mismatches between sexpartite vaulting and the underlying uniform columns at Paris, and a converse mismatch at Noyon. He interprets the differences as probably reflecting changes in scheme during building, but this historical explanation is his argument, not a demonstrated fact. He notes counterarguments by Viollet-le-Duc, who identifies subsidiary reinforcements.

**Application:** A visually adjusted roof cannot fix a lower-arcade count mismatch or an unsupported new transverse spring. C-P2's remaining 2/2/3 arcade rhythm is a fundamental architectural issue, not a mere rendering problem. E2 cannot be accepted until X−4949 has an architecturally plausible and physically supported receiving condition.

### Finding M04 — Preserve ordinary choir rhythm by changing the exceptional vault

**Chapter V, pp. 163–165, figs. 85–88:** Moore criticizes the tripartite terminal adjustment at Noyon for disturbing the continuity of the choir's quadripartite system. At Chartres, supplementary ribs and a shifted apsidal junction allow an independent apsidal vault while preserving uniform choir vaults. At Amiens the arrangement is refined again, with a more satisfactory radial geometry.

**Application:** **This is the leading MP-03 design analogy.** Allow the N10/crossing transition to be special internally, but require the ordinary paired rhythm to remain intelligible on the nave side. A purpose-designed common turning canopy is more promising than merely disguising three separate compartments with a curved ceiling. Different location (apse vs crossing) means analogy, not reproduction.

### Finding M05 — Vault surfaces are governed by ribs and course construction

**Chapter IV, pp. 110–112:** Moore analyzes the Notre-Dame de Paris vaults: different pointedness, stilting and spring elevations lead to a naturally domical and locally irregular surface. He describes masonry courses passing from rib to rib with changing inclinations, rather than a single simple intersecting geometric surface.

**Application:** For later 3D stages, do not solve the N10 canopy by visually smoothing away fixed rib-borne constraints. First settle real carrier geometry, spring levels and supports. This is consistent with V13-derived generator principles, but is not permission to alter V13 now.

### Finding M06 — Curved ambulatory subdivision and ring coordination

**Chapter III, pp. 82–86, figs. 33–35:** Moore contrasts Pontoise, St-Denis, and Sens apsidal aisle vaulting. At St-Denis the opposing diagonal rib branches meet at a plan angle, shifting their junction closer to a useful central location and reducing unequal triangular cells. **Chapter V, pp. 168–170, fig. 91:** he illustrates Notre-Dame de Paris's double ambulatory, in which support points divide inner and outer curved edges differently, producing triangular cells and course directions adapted to the ring geometry. He contrasts a different solution at Le Mans.

**Application:** Valuable for chevet AMB-I / AMB-M / AMB-O and the planned radiating chapels. In particular, the same bay label need not imply identical internal rib subdivisions in concentric bands. **Caution:** these sources are precedents; no existing F5ZK-C or F5ZN-A dimensions should be overwritten.

### Finding M07 — Piers, abaci and upper ribs form one hierarchy

**Chapter IV, pp. 124–128, figs. 53–58:** Moore traces the development of Paris's pier/abacus arrangement as the architectural members respond to unequal loads, independent vaulting shafts, and the emerging articulated pier section. **Chapter VI, pp. 199–205, figs. 107–112:** his comparison of Lincoln and Canterbury includes detailed discussion of crossing piers and pier-vault relationships, although his evaluation of English work is often strongly negative.

**Application:** Particularly relevant to R1 @78°, F5ZK-E seven-service crossing-pier interface, major vs minor responds, and the rule against arbitrary bends and fanning below springing. The receiving member must be set out *from the carriers*, rather than a visually attractive shaft imposed afterward.

### Finding M08 — Transept form and thresholds can be varied

**Chapter V, pp. 171–173:** Moore notes French transepts without side aisles (Paris and Noyon), with aisles on both sides (Chartres, Amiens, Reims), and asymmetrical situations such as Sens. This is a useful check against assuming there is one canonical transept-side rhythm.

**Application:** The N10/Q1/T1 threshold may change spatial character while still retaining hierarchy and identifiable support logic. This complements the project's chosen *grand open nave continuation* and *more intimate choir threshold*, but does not uniquely specify their proportions.

## Historical-source caution

Moore's *1906 second-edition scan* is an interpretive nineteenth-century architectural history reissued in the early twentieth century. He frequently describes certain English and other European traditions as insufficiently “true Gothic,” and tends to evaluate success by structural logic as he defines it. Distinguish (a) observed plans or recorded rib configurations, (b) his proposed histories of alteration, (c) his aesthetic evaluations, and (d) our F5ZP experiments. His figures are not measured 1:1 data for our cathedral.

**Bibliographic note:** The title page identifies second edition rewritten/enlarged, 1906; do not treat a printed figure or page number from a different edition as automatically identical.
