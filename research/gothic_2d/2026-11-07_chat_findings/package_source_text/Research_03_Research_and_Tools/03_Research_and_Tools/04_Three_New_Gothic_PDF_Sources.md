## Source-derived evidence and places to revisit

### A. Parker — church and monastic attachments (1881)

- **PDF p. 61:** In his account of Westminster, Parker discusses the dormitory and refectory alongside successive construction of the abbey church. Relevant to **shared institutional function**, not a precise measured site plan.
- **PDF p. 140 (printed p. 112):** Mentions Salisbury's chapter house while comparing Early English monuments; helps place the feature in a period/style discussion, **not** establish dimensions or attachment geometry.
- **PDF p. 283:** Describes the slender-columned thirteenth-century refectory of Saint-Martin-des-Champs in Paris, with later reuse of monastic remains. Useful as a structural and functional analogy for a distinct refectory.
- **PDF p. 346 (printed p. 286):** Describes a major Florentine religious complex with **multiple cloisters** and a chapter-house entry. Useful for understanding larger institutional precincts, rather than copying one square court.
- **PDF p. 347 (printed p. 287):** Describes a **refectory in a cloister linking to a church** in a Florentine monastic complex. This is unusually pertinent to our question about circulation and adjacency.
- **PDF p. 338 (printed p. 278):** Discusses an earlier monastic atrium/cloister and changed ground levels at San Clemente in Rome; context for medieval accretions, not a Gothic planning rule.

### B. Brandon — cloister connections and Gothic details (1903 ed., Volume I)

- **PDF p. 81 (printed p. 65), “Doorways”:** Uses a **cinquefoiled doorway in the cloisters of Salisbury Cathedral** as an example of mouldings following the foliation while the hood/dripstone follows the larger pointed-arch sweep. A directly relevant doorway design precedent.
- **PDF p. 88 (printed p. 72), “Doorways”:** Describes the **entrance from St Albans cloisters into the church's south aisle**, referring to the interior elevation in **Section I, Perpendicular, Plate 1**. Excellent reference for the cathedral-to-cloister threshold; the cited plate should be visually traced and catalogued before drafting a gateway section.
- **PDF p. 97 (printed p. 81), “Arcades”:** Refers to a **small cloister formerly connecting St Albans church with its abbey buildings** and describes its carved arcade. Relevant to a **short and purposefully integrated architectural link**, as opposed to a long unattached corridor.
- **PDF p. 62 (printed p. 46):** Discusses compass-based geometric derivation of tracery on the doorway into the cloister at St Albans, again citing **Section I, Perpendicular, Plates 1–2**. This is a Gothic set-out resource, not a measured corridor width.

### C. World Architecture Journal — context and vaulting

- **PDF p. 41:** Discusses the evolution of **Gloucester Cathedral's cloister**, its later-medieval fan vaulting, and the distinction between earlier experimental work and later completed work. Useful as a **vault-style comparison**, not an argument to replace F1-H's currently selected quadripartite arrangement.
- **PDF pp. 39–40:** Discusses English Gothic, including Canterbury, Salisbury, Wells and English cathedral characteristics; relevant to differences between English precinct forms and French chevet composition.
- **PDF p. 97:** Places **Salisbury**, its chapter house, and later library in the history of the cathedral. This is secondary historical context; does not supply a measured Salisbury cloister plan.
- **PDF p. 60:** Mentions monasteries and educational buildings in the broader diffusion of Gothic; useful programmatically, but not primary proof for a specific two-building arrangement.

## Research assessment for F5ZP

**Most useful for a cathedral-to-cloister gateway:** Brandon, PDF pp. **81, 88, 97**, followed by Parker p. **347**. They show that Gothic threshold articulation, a cloister/abbey-building connection, and related refectory arrangements merit serious treatment in plan, not simply a dashed route.

**Most useful for cloister construction detail:** Brandon pp. **62, 81, 88** and World Architecture Journal p. **41**. The latter is a caution that English fan vaulting and our chosen French-Gothic-inspired quadripartite vaults are distinct architectural systems, not interchangeable decorations.

**Most useful for campus function:** Parker pp. **61, 283, 346–347**. These examples support investigating **cloister, refectory, chapter house and residences as connected functional components**—including the possibility of multiple courts.

**What these sources do not establish:** Neither a verified **relative floor-plan scaling** for *our* cathedral and library nor an approved **N4/N5/N6 gateway** or 1:1 dimensions for the F5ZP cloister. Use measured Salisbury, Westminster, Durham, and other monastic precinct plans for comparative dimensions; do not resize the cathedral from this source set.
