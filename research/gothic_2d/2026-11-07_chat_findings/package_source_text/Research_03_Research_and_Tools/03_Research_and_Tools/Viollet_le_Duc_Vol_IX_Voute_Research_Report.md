## I. Principal findings

### 1. What *VOÛTE* attempts to explain

[**SOURCE — IX 465**] Viollet-le-Duc states that he treated the general progression of medieval vault construction and resistance to thrust in *CONSTRUCTION*. This article instead concentrates on the means employed between the eleventh and sixteenth centuries **to lay out vaults and establish them on their supports**. It is consequently primarily an essay about *tracé*, construction procedure, ribs, infill and springing arrangements rather than an isolated catalogue of silhouettes.

**Concise original:** « les divers procédés admis en France [...] pour tracer ces voûtes et les établir sur leurs points d'appui » (IX 465). **English:** “the various procedures adopted in France [...] for setting out these vaults and establishing them on their supports.”

**Result for our research:** A comparison restricted to the lower visible curvature of a web would miss much of the argument. We must read the plan, support locations, arch curves, masonry-course directions and transition into springing masonry together.

### 2. The essential distinction is how the vault is built, not whether ribs are visible

[**SOURCE — IX 499–505, especially 502**] Viollet-le-Duc traces Roman vaults with embedded structural brickwork. He distinguishes decorative or reinforcing ribs inserted in an otherwise self-supporting shell from an **independent network of permanent stone arches upon which the individual infill compartments depend**. He is explicit that an apparently ribbed vault is not automatically a *voûte en arcs d'ogive* in this constructional sense. His contrast of Vézelay and Saint-Denis is intended to establish this distinction.

**Concise original:** « considérer ces nerfs [...] comme une membrure indépendante » (IX 502). **English:** “to regard these ribs [...] as an independent framework.”

**Evidence caution:** The proposition that Saint-Denis supplies the first fully developed example is Viollet-le-Duc's historical position, which he defends polemically against F. de Verneilh (IX 503). It is **not independently proven by our two uploaded books**; it should not be presented as an uncontested consensus or as proof that no lost precursors existed.

### 3. The curved ambulatory is the central geometric test case

[**SOURCE — IX 489–498; IV 69–82**] A curved annular passage has unequal openings at its inner and outer edges. If builders continue to rely on semicircular barrels penetrating an annular barrel, their intersections may yield irregular or rising groins, unequal springings, awkward pier imposts, conical elements, displaced arêtes and unused or overloaded parts of the *tailloir*. Viollet-le-Duc compares Notre-Dame-du-Port, Poissy and Brioude, then traces solutions found in the emerging rib-based method.

At Langres (IV 70–72) he describes a wedge-shaped bay whose crown line inclines, and at Notre-Dame de Châlons (IV 75–80) he identifies the expedient of **adding a supporting column so a troublesome trapezoid can be divided into more manageable vault units**. Thus the source does *not* prescribe a single universal rib pattern for every plan: it shows that masters could change support topology as well as adjust vault geometry.

### 4. Saint-Denis: arrange an independent support network, then construct the irregular compartments

[**SOURCE — IX 503–506, fig. 22 on printed p. 504**] For the double ambulatory and radiating chapel at Saint-Denis, Viollet-le-Duc first locates support points and *arcs ogives* geometrically in plan. He then describes how pointed arches give flexibility in the relationship between span, springing height and crown level. The triangular masonry fillings are subsequently constructed on their extrados. One chapel compartment is expressly described as beginning like part of a small dome and then **warping as it rises** to meet the outer surfaces of the window arch and rib.

**Concise original:** « la surface courbe, gauchissant à mesure qu'elle s'élève » (IX 505). **English:** “the curved surface, warping as it rises.” The accompanying perspective and masonry-course sketch show a localized transition, not a rule that the whole bay must be one sphere.

**Crucial caution:** *Gauchissant* is an observation about spatial deformation of the surface. It does **not** determine a unique mathematical surface. Many different surfaces can connect the prescribed boundary curves.

### 5. Notre-Dame de Paris: concentric, radial and diagonal members organize the triangular webs

[**SOURCE — IX 512–513, fig. 26, actually engraved on printed p. 512**] The diagram of a portion of the double ambulatory distinguishes *archivoltes* (D), concentric *arcs-doubleaux* (E), radial *arcs-doubleaux* (F), and diagonal *arcs-doubleaux* (G). The differently sized arches reach different crown elevations. Curved **lignes de clefs bombées** tie the crown region together; the triangular curved masonry compartments are laid **in courses parallel to these lines**, resting at their ends on the extrados of the stone arches. The author goes on to explain marking corresponding divisions along an arch and a curved key-line to direct the courses (IX 513).

This supplies a historically grounded explanation for curved triangular web wings: they are not autonomous patches sculpted toward a guessed dome; they are masonry compartments organized by **the actual arches and crown lines of the bay**. It does not prescribe a numerical bulge percentage or a single web-interpolation formula.

### 6. Web courses: an important French–English divergence

[**SOURCE — IX 521–525, figs. 33–34; IV 103–112**] In Viollet-le-Duc's account, the French method places an **equal number** of construction divisions on the formeret and the longer diagonal rib, making the intervals on the latter physically larger. This directs rows of *moellons piqués* toward a curved line of keys and gives individually concave compartments. He contrasts an English method in which the **actual intervals** along formeret and diagonal are equal, producing differing numbers of intervals, skewing the courses in plan and allowing a simpler, increasingly panel-like fill. Transitional experiments are discussed at Westminster; he regards the English development as a progressively distinct constructive logic, not simply an increase in ornament.

**Implication:** Our generator must separate (a) rib network, (b) crown/key-line network, and (c) **masonry course-direction field**. It must not assume that a visual web surface determines the historically intended bed-joint directions.

### 7. Springing masonry is a structured transition, not a spray of independent bent ribs

[**SOURCE — IX 491–494, 514–516, 538–542; IV 85–94**] The text repeatedly examines how the first stones of several arches can be accommodated on a constrained support. The *tas de charge* may contain horizontal beds and interpenetrating profiles below the level where the arches become fully independent. At Senlis and later examples, the grouping, intersection and departure heights of individual arches determine the appearance and size of responds and piers.

[**PROPOSAL — not yet implemented**] For A4–A6, test a common three-dimensional springing *envelope* and controlled emergence of the ribs before attempting independent sweeps all the way to the pier. This is a way to investigate the historical construction logic, **not authorization to alter the accepted V13 springing**.

### 8. Curved horizontal rib projection is historically documented, but is not automatically required

[**SOURCE — IX 538–542, figs. 41–44**] Viollet-le-Duc analyses Narbonne and Bourges, including a case in which an *arc ogive* has **curvature in horizontal projection**, so that its alignment serves adjacent compartments. This is directly relevant to our concern about lateral web shoulders. However the same article exhibits independent planar rib arrangements elsewhere and does not assert that every rib in a curved ambulatory must depart from a vertical plane. We should treat the curved projection as a documented **alternative family** whose plan geometry and junctions need explicit study, not a mandatory correction to V13.

### 9. The English late-Gothic solution is its own mathematical system

[**SOURCE — IX 526–537, figs. 35–40; IV 110–122**] Later English vaults multiply tiercerons and short ties, but the geometry can become simpler: arcs emerge from the pier using sections of a shared compound curve and are braced by liernes and counter-liernes. The eventual fan/conoid appearance follows from the common-curve, distributed network and panel-like construction. Viollet-le-Duc contrasts short French voussoirs and mortar joints (which he associates with accommodating settlement) with larger, more continuously linked pieces in the English systems.

This is an analytical contrast, not a suitable automatic substitute for a twelfth- or thirteenth-century French ambulatory. In particular, a fan-shaped springing should not be accepted merely because it is easy to parametrize.

### 10. Later French complexity is a warning about over-constrained networks

[**SOURCE — IX 543–550, figs. 45–46**] Viollet-le-Duc criticizes elaborate late French nets whose secondary members impose incompatible heights, different actions, awkward intersections and pendant bosses. Some solutions require deliberately altered rib backs or pendant keystones to accommodate the arrivals. He argues for structural intelligibility over decorative combinations. This judgment is an author's evaluation, sometimes strongly worded; modern engineering verification must be separate. For our work, it flags the danger of allowing uncontrolled secondary curves to create intersections that cannot be supported or sensibly coursed.

### Unit 01–04 findings — Roman work and the medieval inheritance (465–488)

[**SOURCE**] The opening is primarily concerned with the **temporary support required to construct masonry**, not just the final compression shell. Roman builders, according to the author, constructed modest centering, laid brick skins or a series of reinforcing brick ribs, and then completed the intermediate fill. In domes they could organize the centering as radial members; the apparent coffering relates to temporary centering and selective diminution of mass. The discussion of the Pantheon proposes a specific construction sequence but repeatedly has to infer inaccessible details (IX 470–477). In the eastern Mediterranean examples (IX 478–484) the visible arch may correspond more directly to load-bearing structure, and the substitution of masonry vaults for timber roofs changes pier spacing and roof geometry. At the beginning of the Western experiments, the diagonal may reinforce an already essentially domical groin rather than act as the independent carrier of triangular cells.

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] A geometric intersection of cylindrical shells is **not** interchangeable with a rib-and-web construction. The former requires compatible surface intersections, while the latter can place ribs along independently selected three-dimensional lines and subsequently infill the spaces between them. Roman coffering or reinforcing brick is an analogy for rib organization, but a different load-bearing relationship.

### Unit 05–06 findings — curved plan and the failure of a universal barrel method (489–498)

[**SOURCE**] The inner and outer circular passage edges are of different lengths. Viollet-le-Duc explores the consequences of trying to keep semicircular arch forms, horizontal crown lines and square *tailloirs* all at once. One solution makes archivoltes conical; another requires awkward trapezoidal imposts; another changes spring heights and creates groins whose apparent start no longer sits logically in the corner. At Poissy he documents altered projections and an elevated outer web contour. At Brioude he finds exceptionally careful dressed-stone construction that nonetheless remains, in his classification, Romanesque in principle.

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] The problem is **over-constraint**: the same four supports are being asked to satisfy span-dependent semicircular heights, uniform apex elevations, stable impost bearing and clean groin intersections. The Gothic approach releases some of these simultaneous conditions by prescribing separately the rib network, arch heights and infill geometry.

### Unit 07 findings — Saint-Denis (499–505)

[**SOURCE**] Figure 22 combines a geometrical plan, schematic arch profiles, perspective and courses of web masonry. The disposition of the chapel window is not symmetric relative to the vault compartment because a buttress intervenes. The lower infill begins with dome-like coursing but changes its surface as it climbs toward the rib and window extrados. The author explicitly observes different springing levels for chapel versus ambulatory ribs. The system is not simply a planar patch hung between straight boundary arcs.

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] Treat each web cell as having (i) a planar domain, (ii) a boundary in three-dimensional space prescribed by arches and adjoining masonry, (iii) a crown/key-line boundary when present, and (iv) a **course-direction field**. The boundaries generally do **not** uniquely determine the interior surface. An additional historical or modeling criterion is needed; do not assert that a simple bilinear or spherical patch is Viollet-le-Duc's rule.

### Unit 08–11 findings — Sens, Paris and later rib networks (506–520)

[**SOURCE**] At Sens the surviving construction and later alterations force reconstruction; the author gives reasons for some conclusions and acknowledges uncertainty on original masonry laying (IX 508–511). At Paris, figure 26 furnishes the strongest example of actual annular and radial arch relationships and their curved triangular compartments (IX 512–513). Springings at Senlis increasingly interpenetrate until the arches separate above shared courses (IX 515–516). By the thirteenth century, reducing excessively domical crowns permits more compact roofing and motivates adding tiercerons and liernes to broad bays (IX 517–520).

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] A crown-level adjustment has consequences throughout the building: roof clearance, clerestory openings, web course heights, sidewall/counter-thrust and the architecture of the springing. Thus a generator must not treat “bulge” as an entirely free visual knob disconnected from supporting arches and crown conditions.

### Unit 12–14 findings — contrasting masonry fields (521–537)

[**SOURCE**] The course-setting differences are clearly described at IX 521–524, and the English geometric simplifications at IX 526–535. In the French examples, the compartments retain distinct curved *voûtains*; the English progression uses many interlocked arcs and tends toward relatively simple surface segments or dressed-stone panels. Viollet-le-Duc explicitly stresses constructive reasons for the rib geometry and objects to mystical explanations of compound curves (IX 534–535).

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] A programmable course field has independent degrees of freedom. Equal **count** of divisions on unlike borders and equal **spacing** of divisions are mathematically different. The resulting joint trajectories can be visually and structurally distinct even when a coarse surface mesh looks similar.

### Unit 15–18 findings — chevet springings and constructive limits (538–550)

[**SOURCE**] Narbonne and Bourges supply details of how arch members and vertical responds negotiate the transition from parallel bays to *travées gironnantes*; the plan-curved ogive at Bourges is tied to masonry organization rather than ornament for its own sake (IX 539–541). At Saint-Ouen, formeres and diagonals penetrate into tall, concentrated pier springings, with horizontal bedding carried higher (IX 541–542). A later French net at Saint-Florentin contains junctions requiring complex pendant stones and changing rib depth near intersections (IX 545–548). The final assessment is clearly polemical (IX 549–550), and not a measured failure survey of every Renaissance vault.

[**RECONSTRUCTION**] A visually continuous web wing must be checked where it meets a doubleau: vertical elevation along the joining line, tangent alignment (where desired), coursing direction, bedding continuity and available body of stone. A curved-in-plan rib family could be tested, but the source does not authorize an automatic departure from V13's accepted planar ogives.

### Three important cross-checks

**A. Irregular annular geometry is described twice, with complementary emphasis.** IV 69 explicitly contrasts the larger inner/outer groin openings of a curving passage and the necessity of altering arch heights; IX 490–494 concentrates on the geometry of archivoltes and the bearing available on the impost. The two passages support the diagnosis of over-constrained intersections, without proving that any particular modern Bézier or spline surface would be authentic.

**B. Plan topology can change instead of forcing a distorted surface.** IV 75–76 says the builder of Notre-Dame de Châlons introduced columns so a problematic trapezoid could be vaulted as a parallelogram plus a triangle. This is a significant counterexample to a naive interpretation of “floor-plan independent”: historical designers sometimes **changed the plan** to make the vault rational.

**C. Construction courses, not merely smoothness, define the web.** IV 103–108 gives operational guidance on marking corresponding divisions and using light movable centering while placing stones. IX 521–524 makes the contrasting division strategy explicit. The visible vault surface must be tested against a plausible family of courses and joints, not optimized for smoothness alone.

### The mechanical language needs historical discipline

[**SOURCE — IV 63–65**] Viollet-le-Duc invokes the *courbe des pressions* and the resultant oblique thrust. He describes an arch as sound when the pressure line remains within its thickness, and explains how counter-thrust can balance thrust without causing it to vanish. He also says medieval builders did **not** necessarily know the algebraic formulation available to nineteenth-century engineers.

[**CONTEXT / LIMITATION**] A line-of-thrust check is a valuable modern idealization for compressive masonry, but we should not mistake nineteenth-century mechanics as a literal record of a twelfth-century designer's calculations. A precise assessment of an individual stone vault would require loads, joints, stiffness/settlement assumptions, masonry density, supports, geometry and perhaps advanced numerical analysis. None of those parameters is fully given for V13 in the source.

### Saint-Denis (IX 503–505, fig. 22): reconstruction questions

Figure 22's A is a radial chapel and double ambulatory plan; the lettered rib branches, point locations and their schematic elevation are specific to **that** chapel scheme. The off-center window opening is constrained by a buttress. The irregular lower web patch follows a dome-like start and a progressively warped connection toward a window arch and diagonal rib. The *course plan* is part of the explanation; a smooth 3D patch that omits those courses could reproduce the silhouette but not the construction logic.

To make a genuine geometric reconstruction we would have to measure from the original drawing: support points, arch and window spans, crown levels, the line of keys, and the actual orientation of the courses at at least several locations. A scan of a perspective engraving is **not an exact scale survey**. Coordinates extracted from it should be labeled **illustrative only**, unless the plate's dimensional scheme provides a trustworthy internal scale.

### Notre-Dame de Paris (IX 512–513, fig. 26): reconstruction questions

The lettered classification in the printed text is unusually precise: **D** = archivoltes; **E** = concentric doubleaux; **F** = radial doubleaux; **G** = diagonal doubleaux. The text separately describes **d/e/f/g** arch summits and elevated **h** locations in the key-line network. Web units are triangular in plan but curved in space, and the courses are indexed to the upper lines and the extrados of their stone supports. The figure is a **diagram of geometry plus construction**, not a smooth-render proposal.

The model must preserve two distinctions: a diagonal masonry member is *not* a bisector of an arbitrary triangular surface by definition, and the *ligne de clefs* need not be a horizontal straight line through a single equal-elevation boss. An ambulatory can contain several adjacent crown relationships whose heights are selected by constructional constraints.

### Narbonne / Bourges / Saint-Ouen (IX 538–542, figs. 41–44)

These images are especially valuable for separating **pier/respond geometry** from **vault web geometry**. The position of a cylindrical pier, the width of its archivolte, the level of emergence of a radial *doubleau* and the horizontal movement of a rib in plan can jointly determine whether a transition reads as a coherent architectural system. The Bourges passage is the clearest counterexample to the assumption that an *arc ogive* must always project to a straight line on the plan. The Saint-Ouen example warns that the first section of several ribs may be inseparable within a tall block of common springing masonry even though the mature arches diverge above it.

## V. Modern mathematical reconstructions — explicitly NOT formulas quoted from Viollet-le-Duc

The book provides geometrical drawings, practical methods, and dimensional relationships, **not** a complete symbolic algorithm for arbitrary modern meshes. The following gives a testable **modern translation** of selected constraints. These formulas are our reconstruction and must never be cited as his explicit equations.

### A. Idealized annular bay as a first model

Let an annular sector have inner radius **rᵢ**, outer radius **rₒ**, and angular width **Δθ** in radians. Its radial depth is **d = rₒ − rᵢ**. The straight chord across its inside opening is

`L_inner = 2 rᵢ sin(Δθ/2)`

and across its outside opening is

`L_outer = 2 rₒ sin(Δθ/2)`.

The outer/inner chord ratio is `rₒ/rᵢ`, **not one**. A diagonal connecting the inner corner on one radial line to the outer corner on the other has length

`L_diag = sqrt(rᵢ² + rₒ² − 2 rᵢ rₒ cos(Δθ))`.

This idealization ignores offsets, pierced supports, uneven bay angles, buttress projections and asymmetries in a real chevet. It nonetheless proves why a rectangular one-span template cannot preserve all inner, outer and diagonal arch conditions without adjustment. In a **nonconcentric** or **nonuniform** bay, calculate actual chord and diagonal lengths directly from the two-dimensional support coordinates instead of assuming radial symmetry.

### B. Independently chosen pointed circular arch

For a symmetric two-centered pointed arch with spring points `(0,zₛ)` and `(L,zₛ)`, and apex `(L/2,zₛ+H)`, an idealized circular left branch with center `(c,zₛ)` is determined by

`c = L/4 + H²/L`.

For `0 ≤ x ≤ L/2` the upper branch is

`z(x) = zₛ + sqrt(c² − (x−c)²)`,

and the other half is its mirror. For the equilateral pointed form `H = (sqrt(3)/2)L`, this gives `c=L`. This is a **contemporary analytic parameterization** of a pointed circular arch, not a universal prescription taken from the book. The form becomes semicircular at `H=L/2`; not all historic arches have the same rise, center or springing geometry.

For V13, the independently controlled quantities remain *support coordinates, plan projection of the rib, spring height, crown/boss height, arch family and moulding*. An attempted update should not recalculate a protected rib simply because a web patch changes.

### C. Plan-curved rib family: geometry separate from height

If a rib is curved in plan, define its horizontal path `γ(s)=(x(s),y(s))` by measured control points or a historically defensible path. Define vertical height `z(s)` separately. The three-dimensional curve is then `R(s)=(x(s),y(s),z(s))`. Its tangent is `(x′(s),y′(s),z′(s))`. A **planar** rib is the special case in which `γ(s)` is a straight horizontal line; plan curvature is not introduced merely by making the height profile ogival.

A future **experimental** comparison could compare the planar V13 variant with one plan-curved Bourges-inspired variant using **identical springing and boss heights and preserved profiles**. The book does not give a general curvature parameter for such a sweep, so any selected control points would be modern hypotheses.

### D. Crown/key-line and course indexing

Let `A(u)` be a three-dimensional line on the extrados of one supporting arch and `B(u)` a relevant crown or second boundary line, with `0≤u≤1` indexed in order of the *masonry courses*. In a simple schematic, course k connects `A(k/n)` to `B(k/n)` for k=0,…,n. This **equal-count** indexing is a modern way to express the French passage described at IX 521: n course intervals on each of two unequal-length curves. Their physical widths need not match.

An **equal-distance** family instead places stations at `s = k a` in arc length along each respective curve, so the number of stations generally differs if the curve lengths differ. Intersections or overlap near the key line must then be resolved. This abstracts the English comparison at IX 522–524, again without claiming that every English vault uses one formula.

A continuous display surface may be interpolated as `S(u,v)` between prescribed three-dimensional boundary curves, but **boundaries alone do not determine a unique surface or coursing**. A bilinear interpolant, spherical cap, conoid, spline or minimal-curvature patch could share the same edges while behaving very differently in the interior. The authentic study therefore needs an additional source-based criterion: course directions, measured transverse rise, stone bedding and/or the drawn key-line network.

### E. A constrained, reversible web experiment (not an implementation)

One candidate numerical scheme for **exploration** is to retain the approved ribs as immovable boundaries and solve for an infill surface whose boundary error is zero. A regularization functional could penalize unrealistic abrupt changes of curvature, but it must also respect any source-derived key lines and course trajectories. A generic illustrative objective is

`E = w_b E_boundary + w_k E_keylines + w_c E_coursing + w_s E_smoothness`.

Here the first three terms should dominate any purely visual smoothness. This objective is our contemporary numerical design tool, not a claim about masonry equilibrium. Separate tests would then investigate whether courses have plausible support at their ends; a visual optimization **cannot** establish compressive structural safety.

**Important:** No source passage requires our existing 5.5% V13 bulge, a fixed polynomial dome, or a uniform amount of sag across A4–A6. Those are modern generator properties and should be validated as such.

### F. Structural verification is a separate phase

A minimal mechanical study would later approximate masonry density, vault thickness, point and distributed loads, line-of-thrust boundaries, support reactions and horizontal thrust. It should account for voussoir/contact behavior and support movements if available. We can derive geometric feasibility and course continuity before doing such a study, but cannot certify masonry stability from the uploaded illustrated treatise alone.

## VIII. Evidence register and remaining uncertainties

**Highest-confidence observations directly anchored in the uploaded source:** IX 465 (purpose); IX 489–494 (curved-passages over-constraint); IX 502 (independent framework criterion); IX 503–505 and fig. 22 (Saint-Denis radial chapel and warp); IX 512–513 and fig. 26 (Paris triangular web and crown/course network); IX 521–524 and figs. 33–34 (French–English course difference); IX 538–542 and figs. 41–44 (chevet springings and curved-plan ogive); IV 69–76 (Langres/Châlons plan and topology); IV 85–94 (springing masonry); IV 103–108 (course layout and centering).

**Partly historical hypotheses belonging to Viollet-le-Duc rather than established independently here:** precise origin of Gothic structural invention; diffusion from Syrian/Byzantine precedents; archaeological datings proposed for Sens and various choir campaigns; the specific reconstructed Roman/ Pantheon centering method; claims about entire national traditions being intrinsically rational or irrational; universal mechanical conclusions about sixteenth-century vault failures. Our task is to understand his reasoning, then evaluate those claims against the actual architectural evidence rather than treat historical confidence as uniform.

**Translation caveat:** the 1868 printed text includes spellings and usage that do not map perfectly onto current English architectural language. This report deliberately retains important French terms. Very brief original phrases were checked against the page text; any future publication-quality quotation or reconstruction should be proofed line by line against the rendered scan, particularly on pages occupied chiefly by plates or pages where the automated text layer is broken.

**Geometric gaps not filled by the uploads:** exact surveyed coordinate tables for Saint-Denis and Paris; the entire three-dimensional form of every web from a single diagram; a universal rib-rise formula; masonry material properties; an invariant cathedral-wide web-bulge percentage; and a demonstrated conversion from the author's lettered diagrams to Minecraft/Axiom block positions. We can research and reconstruct these with declared assumptions; we cannot honestly claim they were stated in the treatise.

### Historical principle to carry forward

The defensible common element is **architectural separation of functions**: piers and springers receive a deliberately drawn network of arches; arches establish the surfaces and edges on which small masonry webs are laid; crown lines, course directions and the surrounding architectural plan must agree. Curved ambulatories are a critical proving ground because unequal spans and irregular support positions expose inconsistencies that a rectangular four-part vault may conceal.

**Research conclusion:** The uploaded text gives us a strong historical basis for a **constraint-driven, plan-aware Gothic vault system**. It does **not** prove that a uniquely correct mathematically smooth surface can be inferred from floor plan alone. Our next discussion should center on the **Saint-Denis figure 22**, **Paris figure 26** and **Bourges/Narbonne figures 41–44**, before deciding whether any test of A4–A6 should use web-only changes, curved-in-plan ribs, or springing improvements. Until that discussion, **V13 remains unchanged**.

## IX. Translation dossier — consequential technical passages

Each passage below gives a **short, source-based French extract** (with word-spacing and line-breaks regularized, but without inventing missing content), a close English translation and a limited inference. The quoted French is an excerpt rather than a transcription of the entire paragraph. For any academic quotation, consult the original scan at the cited printed page.

**T1 — Purpose and method (IX 465).** French: « développer les divers procédés admis en France du XIe au XVIe siècle pour tracer ces voûtes et les établir sur leurs points d'appui. » English: “to explain the various procedures used in France from the eleventh to the sixteenth century to lay out these vaults and establish them on their bearing points.” **Reading:** the article has an operational construction aim, not an exclusively formal one.

**T2 — The ambulatory as a design problem (IX 489).** French: « les architectes occidentaux ne cessèrent de chercher la solution de ce problème » and « ces voûtes pourtournant les sanctuaires ». English: “Western architects never ceased seeking a solution to this problem” and “these vaults encircling sanctuaries.” **Reading:** the author intentionally presents the turning ambulatory as a central reason to follow the history of vault geometry.

**T3 — Empirical difficulty of distorted barrels (IX 491).** French: « Des voûtes ainsi conçues ne pouvaient être tracées sur l'épure avec rigueur; on ne les obtenait que par des tâtonnements et une méthode empirique. » English: “Vaults conceived in this way could not be rigorously set out in the full-size drawing; they were obtained only through trial adjustments and an empirical method.” **Reading:** the problem is not merely unattractive appearance, but insufficient determinacy for stonecutting and setting out.

**T4 — Independent rib structure (IX 502).** French: « Mais considérer ces nerfs, ressortis de la voûte, comme une membrure indépendante [...] c'est alors un nouveau principe qui s'établit ». English: “But to regard these ribs, drawn out from the vault, as an independent framework [...] is to establish a new principle.” **Reading:** the decisive distinction concerns the dependency of masonry webs on an independently established structure. The quotation's ellipsis omits the author's description of webs carried by that framework; it does not omit a contrary qualification.

**T5 — Plan and vault as reciprocal decisions (IX 503).** French: « c'était le système de voûtes à employer qui lui donnait les dispositions de son plan. » English: “it was the system of vaulting to be used that gave him the arrangement of his plan.” **Reading:** Viollet-le-Duc does not frame the design process as a purely automatic “generate a vault from any fixed floor plan” operation; floor plan and vault system inform each other.

**T6 — Warped chapel filling at Saint-Denis (IX 505).** French: « Le remplissage voûté commence comme commencerait une coupole sur une partie circulaire; puis la surface courbe, gauchissant à mesure qu'elle s'élève, va chercher l'extrados de l'archivolte et celui de la branche d'arc ogive. » English: “The vaulted infill begins as a dome would begin over a circular portion; then the curved surface, warping as it rises, reaches the extrados of the archivolt and that of the diagonal rib branch.” **Reading:** there is a spatial transition from a near-domical lower portion into **specified structural boundaries**, not a free-standing decorative wing.

**T7 — Paris: the curved lines of keys (IX 512).** French: « il a réuni les extrados des points culminants des arcs F et G par des courbes ou lignes de clefs bombées ». English: “he joined the extradoses of the highest points of arches F and G by curves, or domed lines of keys.” **Reading:** the crest/crown network is a separate geometric control. The word *bombées* should not be converted directly into a fixed percentage of modern web bulge.

**T8 — Paris: coursing on the arches (IX 512–513).** French: « par rangs parallèles à ces lignes de clefs »; « ces triangles concaves viennent-ils reposer leur poids sur les arcs de pierre ». English: “in courses parallel to these key-lines”; “these concave triangles thus bring their weight to rest upon the stone arches.” **Reading:** the course field and its points of support are integral to the author's idea of the web.

**T9 — French course division (IX 521).** French: « sur l'extrados de la courbe du formeret et sur l'extrados de la courbe de l'arc ogive un nombre égal de divisions ». English: “an equal number of divisions on the outer curve of the formeret and the outer curve of the diagonal rib.” **Reading:** equal number of course intervals is not equal physical interval width; the formeret and ogive have unequal developed lengths.

**T10 — English course division (IX 522).** French: « non plus en un nombre égal de divisions, mais en divisions égales ». English: “no longer into an equal number of divisions, but into equal-sized divisions.” **Reading:** this is the crucial difference between the two masonry-course systems explained in figs. 33–34.

**T11 — Curved horizontal projection (IX 539).** French: « Afin de ne pas avoir en e un angle trop aigu, le constructeur a donné, en projection horizontale, une courbure à l'arc ogive eC. » English: “In order not to have too acute an angle at e, the builder gave the diagonal rib eC a curvature in horizontal projection.” **Reading:** the original motive is a more useful relation between adjacent fill triangles, not arbitrary waviness in an already approved planar rib.

**T12 — The French web remains a vaulted cell (IX 541).** French: « les arcs remplissent toujours les fonctions de cintres permanents recevant des voûtains de remplissage entre leurs branches ». English: “the arches continue to perform the function of permanent centering, receiving the infill vaultlets between their branches.” **Reading:** this later summary makes explicit the continuing role of separate vault cells in the French line of development.

**T13 — Springing integration (IX 539).** French: « la section de la pile n'est autre que la section de l'archivolte ». English: “the section of the pier is none other than the section of the archivolt.” **Reading:** in the author's Saint-Ouen example the support profile is directly related to arch profiles; this supports careful respond/arch alignment, not inventing independent downturned mouldings.
