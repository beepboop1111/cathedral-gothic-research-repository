# J. E. Paynter — Practical Geometry for Builders and Architects (1921)
## Complete-book research reading for Gothic construction and tracery

**Source:** The user-supplied `practicalgeometr00paynrich.pdf` (432 scanned PDF pages; 1921 Chapman & Hall edition).

**Review method and limits:** Every PDF page was indexed for text and included in a twelve-sheet visual contact survey. Twenty-five mathematically or architecturally important pages were rendered individually at higher resolution. The text layer is historical OCR: some equations, diagram letters, and captions are garbled and must always be checked against the printed page when used as precise evidence. This is a **source-grounded construction-method guide**, not a claim that the book documents all of our cathedral's measurements. Source illustrations remain unchanged.

**Page reference rule:** In the main body, *PDF page = printed page + 16*. Thus printed page 155 is PDF page 171. Preliminary pages 1–16 precede the printed page 1. The last seven PDF pages contain publisher advertisements and library matter.

## Central conclusion

Paynter's method is **set-out first, finished form second**. He frequently shows the guiding axes, centers, line divisions, intersection points, and construction arcs separately from a finished design. This is directly relevant to the user's preference: **begin with dividing lines; derive centers and polygons; calculate angles from measured points; use limited, deliberate rotation; verify the resulting layer before repeating it**.

The book does **not** establish that Paynter's sample motifs are the original historical methods for any particular Ungewitter, Haina, or cathedral drawing. Applying these constructions to other plates is a **research hypothesis** until source-specific evidence confirms the relationship.

## Especially important historical constructions

### 1. Chapter II: recover the angle from actual measured points (pp. 14–19)

Paynter defines the tangent ratio from a *right-angled triangle* as perpendicular divided by base (printed pp. 15, 17–19; Figs. 11, 15, 18). His examples use a triangle to recover an angle from measured side lengths, not just to assign an angle by visual resemblance.

For our figure with two points **O** (central origin) and **P** (measured vertex), let `dx = Px - Ox` and `dy = Py - Oy` in a fixed Cartesian frame. The angle from +x is `atan2(dy, dx)`; the **signed** angle between two rays is `atan2(cross(v1,v2), dot(v1,v2))`. For an acute angle from vertical in a correctly right-triangulated projection, `atan(|dx|/|dy|)` gives the magnitude. Avoid directly dividing lengths of **non-perpendicular** colored lines, which is not the tangent ratio described in the book.

**Application to user's sexfoil:** the first 30° is an exact half-step between 60° primary axes *only for ideal sixfold symmetry*. Measured source centers may depart slightly; report both measured deviation and ideal half-step, and do **one** source copy at 30° before deciding whether a derived layer is meaningful.

### 2. Chapter IV: regulating polygons (pp. 45–60)

These methods explain construction of regular polygons and the distinction between geometric means and arithmetic means. For a fitted six-center sexfoil, produce both:

- The *measured* six-vertex polygon, with each vertex traceable to a separately fitted lobe center.
- The *best-fit ideal* regular hexagon, with residuals and a recorded fitting method.

Connecting center points is a discovery **operation**. That does not prove the printed construction used those same lines unless a guide, mark, or consistent tangency independently supports it.

### 3. Chapter VI and VII: circles, tangencies, and center loci (pp. 73–109)

A true circular arc has a recoverable center/radius, and the possible centers of tangent circles form loci. These are the fundamental operations for reconstructing foliated Gothic windows and identifying open vs touching arcs. Record both geometric intersections and *tangent* contacts; a visually close approach is not automatically a tangency.

### 4. Chapter X: Gothic construction drawings (pp. 145–160)

- **Fig. 183, printed pp. 145–146** — repeated circles and semicircles on underlying square/rectangular grids, including circles arranged to intersect at six equally spaced points.
- **Fig. 184, printed pp. 147–150** — repeat patterns for banding/rails, including tangent, concentric and interlacing arrangements, some creating recurring quatrefoils.
- **Fig. 187, printed pp. 153–154** — hexagonal ceiling panels regulated by interlacing equilateral triangles and central stars; a clear example of one simple geometric framework producing a more intricate ornament.
- **Fig. 188, printed p. 154** — a four-stage progression from four interlacing circles to more detailed ornamental patterns. It is a powerful precedent for *adding construction layers* rather than simply subdividing angle after angle.
- **Fig. 189, printed pp. 155–156** — four finished circular Gothic windows together with corresponding **smaller set-out sketches**. Paynter describes dividing a diameter, placing semicircle centers, drawing a 30° axis and related bisectors, then placing centers for progressively smaller arcs. He also describes threefold and fourfold alternatives. Use one figure's small construction sketch as the authority for the corresponding finished figure; do not mix unrelated panels.
- **Fig. 190, printed pp. 156–159** — a triangular window constructed through equilateral triangles, bisections, inscribed circles and points governing stonework; then a square window with a central quatrefoil and differently constructed corner tracery.
- **Fig. 191, printed pp. 158–160** — geometrically constructed column entasis (parabolic, elliptical, hyperbolic). Relevant beyond tracery for accurate shaft, pinnacle and pier profile modeling.

**Source vs extrapolation:** Paynter provides **historical examples of his own pedagogy** and set-out steps. Our idea of overlaying a whole foil once at the half-step angle, harvesting derived intersection networks and iterating into multiple rose-window layers is a separate algorithmic interpretation. Do not attribute that general algorithm to Paynter without direct textual support.

### 5. Chapter XIV: exact moulding intersections (pp. 245–249)

Paynter explicitly distinguishes a **raking mould** from its **return mould** and shows how the correct section changes with inclination. This is central to our earlier incorrect fan-shaped arch/respond transitions. Instead of rotating a PNG silhouette indiscriminately, generate the required transformed section in the plane where the stone surfaces intersect and compare its boundary against the retained profile.

### 6. Chapter XXII: forces and early buttress research (pp. 339–370)

Use Paynter's graphic-statics constructions as transparent visual checks: force polygons, resolved components, funicular polygons and centroid lines. A valid geometric force diagram is **not** evidence that a Gothic buttress has sufficient strength, correct foundation behavior or sound 3D masonry bonding. Historical method, derived ideal model, and engineering verification must remain separate.

## Suggested integrated Gothic tracery workflow

1. **Identify the exact printed plate, figure and source scale.** Preserve a read-only original image and its checksum.
2. **Locate true center, dividing lines and point labels before curve fitting.** Distinguish visible ink from reconstructed continuations.
3. **Measure independently.** Recover circle centers, radius, arch apexes, contact points, edges, and recording uncertainty.
4. **Construct point networks.** Connect verified centers and intersections to candidate triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, bisectors, inscribed and circumscribed circles.
5. **Derive angles from vectors and trigonometry.** Store measured value and geometric ideal separately, including sign, reference direction and confidence.
6. **Generate ONE deliberate rotated copy.** For the sexfoil, 30° is the initial ideal half-step; do not automatically branch to 15° or 7.5°.
7. **Evaluate contact geometry.** Catalog source-confirmed intersections, source-compatible new junctions, accidental crossings and forbidden clashes; retain only useful second-layer candidates.
8. **Isolate a representative construction module.** Build measured radii, 2D analytic SVG/DXF and an explicit construction graph before any 3D sweep.
9. **Only after 2D approval:** derive a moulded stone cross-section, trace depth/scale provenance, sweep and resolve real multi-surface joints using the Chapter XIV/XVI concepts.
10. **Keep separate source, derived geometry and architectural authority.** Nothing automatically enters F5ZP locked geometry or its existing generators.
