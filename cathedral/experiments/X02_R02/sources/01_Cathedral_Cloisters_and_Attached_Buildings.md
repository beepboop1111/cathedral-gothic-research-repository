# Research question and status

**Question.** How should a cathedral's cloister connect to the church, chapter house, residence, refectory, library and other institutional buildings—without the precinct becoming a city or compromising the cathedral's exterior? **Status: preliminary report, not a design lock.** The user's newly found book titles/files have not yet been supplied; the first two books examined here are **already in the project's source collection**: Viollet-le-Duc, *Dictionnaire raisonné*, tome III, entry **Cloître**, and Auguste Choisy, *Historia de la arquitectura*, tomo II (2023 Spanish edition).

# 1. Source books already in the project

## Eugène-Emmanuel Viollet-le-Duc — *Dictionnaire raisonné*, Volume III, “Cloître”

**Primary-source location:** uploaded Vol. III PDF, physical pages **418–465** approximately (printed pages begin at 408 and continue through the article). His discussion on physical PDF p. **419**, printed p. **409**, describes cathedral cloisters attached to the **north or south flank of a nave** and associated with the dwellings of canons; schools could also be nearby. This is significant: **neither north nor south is inherently excluded by Gothic precedent.** The statement is the author's historical synthesis, not a claim that every surviving cathedral has an extant cloister.

On physical PDF p. **452** (printed p. **442**), his Noyon Cathedral cloister example, figs. **27–28**, is more directly constructive. The garden-side arcade arch acts as a **formeret** of the rib vault, and wall-side vault bearings are received by **sculpted corbels** rather than projecting engaged piers—explicitly to avoid impeding circulation. The lesson for F1-H is to compare **wall corbels versus deep wall responds**, subject to masonry and load checks, before widening the 3.5 m passage again. He compares Noyon with Semur (fig. 26). See also [Wikisource, tome III, printed p. 442](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Viollet-le-Duc_-_Dictionnaire_raisonn%C3%A9_de_l%E2%80%99architecture_fran%C3%A7aise_du_XIe_au_XVIe_si%C3%A8cle%2C_1854-1868,_tome_3.djvu/445).

**Important distinction:** the French author's cloister design logic helps our geometrical research, but it does not validate our provisional .48 m garden piers, .80 m corner supports, or any particular vault height.

## Auguste Choisy — *Historia de la arquitectura*, Tome II

**Primary-source location:** uploaded 2023 Spanish edition, printed pp. **674–675** (physical PDF pp. **320–321**), especially fig. **16.162**, comparing Montmajour Abbey and Laon Cathedral cloisters. Choisy distinguishes Romanesque cloister vault types from Gothic **rib vaults with buttressed supports**, and discusses traceried glazed or unglazed openings. This helps separate **cloister bay and vault geometry** from the size of an entire monastic building.

A second, especially useful passage is printed pp. **700–701** (physical PDF pp. **346–347**), with fig. **17.16**, comparing the idealized plan of **St Gall** with **Clairvaux**. Choisy discusses religious buildings around the church, dormitories related to the choir, refectory, kitchen, scriptorium and library, guests, infirmary and gardens. In his comparison **Clairvaux has two cloisters**, one of which he characterizes as reserved for study. This supports *researching* a principal religious cloister plus a second scholarly cloister—but it is an abbey comparison, not a universal cathedral formula.

# 2. Comparative case studies

The **octagonal chapter house** is reached from the east cloister walk **through a two-bay vestibule**; the library was built **above the east walk in 1445**. [Durham cloister](https://www.durhamcathedral.co.uk/explore/the-cathedral-building-and-grounds/cloisters); [Durham dormitory](https://www.durhamcathedral.co.uk/explore/the-cathedral-building-and-grounds/the-monks-dormitory). | Most direct example of the **cloister as a circulation hub** rather than an isolated building set in a garden. |
| **Westminster Abbey** | Cloister walks of about **100 ft** apiece lead to functional spaces: **east** to chapter house and dormitory/library, **south** to refectory, **north** historically to study. A *Dark Cloister* joins the *Little Cloister* and former infirmary area. [Westminster Abbey](https://www.westminster-abbey.org/history/explore-our-history/the-cloisters). | Strong precedent for **hierarchical circulation** and a secondary intimate cloister/passage system. |
| **Gloucester Cathedral** | The east cloister walk's famous **fan vault** developed c. 1360–64; other walks were completed later. [Tracing the Past project](https://www.tracingthepast.org.uk/2021/04/07/gloucester_site_by_site/). | Important for vaulting comparisons—but **not** evidence that F1-H's ordinary quadripartite ribs should be replaced by fan vaults. |

# 3. What this changes for the F5ZP project

**Finding A — adjacency before floor area.** In Salisbury, Durham and Westminster, the principal cloister is closely integrated into the church and ranges of adjacent rooms. The earlier **23.8 m distance** from our locked Monastic House to the nearest cathedral masonry/root is not necessarily appropriate for the desired architectural identity. It is a design dimension to review, not a technical requirement.

**Finding B — separate the cloister, house, and library.** A cloister is a system of covered walks and a garth; it need not equal the footprint of a single rectangular “Monastic House.” Historical complexes attach chapter house, service ranges, library and accommodation to specific walks. This suggests testing *attached ranges* while keeping a **distinct, larger Great Library** elsewhere in the precinct.

**Finding C — northern placement deserves study.** Viollet-le-Duc explicitly recognizes cathedral cloisters on either side of a nave. A north-side option must still check shade, external Gothic supports, north-transept access and views. We should compare **north and south** against the approved cathedral geometry before unlocking E5-C3-L1.

**Finding D — two cloisters can have different programs.** The Choisy abbey comparison and Westminster's Little Cloister support studying a **principal contemplative/community cloister** and **smaller scholar-oriented cloister**. They do not require connecting all spaces by continuous corridors.

**Finding E — preserve a hierarchy of access.** Model a cathedral-to-cloister threshold; community circulation around the garth; a protected link to the library; separate public library access; and noninterfering deliveries/archives circulation. Distinguish private from public routes at the entrance, not in the middle of the cloister.

# 4. Questions that remain open

1. Does the Great Cloister attach directly to the **south or north nave**, or meet it through a **short, vaulted vestibule range**?
2. Can we place a chapter house as a **true exterior attachment to an east cloister walk**, rather than forcing it into a narrow perimeter room?
3. What is a defensible ratio between cathedral length, cloister garden, walk width, and the combined monastic ranges? Comparable dimensions must be taken from *measured* plans.
4. Which vault system is best for F1-H: current 30-cell quadripartite conception, or a historically grounded variant with shallower wall supports? No structural decision yet.
5. How should the Great Library and scholar cloister connect without sacrificing spatial openness, landscape, and privacy?
